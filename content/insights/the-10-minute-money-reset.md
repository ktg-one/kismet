Most people avoid looking at their numbers because they expect it to feel stressful. Here's what actually happens when they sit down and do it: clarity. Not panic. Not shame. Just clarity. And clarity almost always feels better than guessing.

The reset is not a budget. It's not a spreadsheet with 14 tabs. It's three habits, ten minutes, once a fortnight. That's it.

## Habit one: know what's coming in

Write down everything hitting your account in the next two weeks. Salary. Rental income. Any side work. Government payments. Whatever it is, get it on paper or into a note on your phone.

Most people skip this step because they think they already know. They don't. Or they do in a rough way, but "roughly" is where the problems start. Knowing your actual fortnightly inflow takes about two minutes and gives you a real foundation to work from.

## Habit two: know what's going out

Same process. Write down every expense you're expecting in the next two weeks. Mortgage or rent. Phone. Power. Groceries. Petrol. Subscriptions. Credit card minimum if there is one.

This is where it gets interesting. Most people are surprised by this number, and not pleasantly. But surprised is still better than blindsided. If you know a shortfall is coming, you can plan around it. If you don't know, it just happens to you.

## Habit three: review every fortnight

Do this again in two weeks. Same process. Ten minutes. The first time feels uncomfortable. The second time feels manageable. By the fourth or fifth time, it's just part of how you operate.

The fortnightly cadence is deliberate. Monthly is too long. You can drift badly in a month and not see it until the damage is done. Weekly is too frequent for most people to sustain. Fortnightly sits in the middle: frequent enough to catch problems early, easy enough to keep doing.

## The five mistakes that quietly cost people

**Yearly bills catching you off-side.** Car registration, insurance renewals, school fees. They are not surprises. They happen every year. The fix is simple: add them up, divide by 26, and treat that amount as a fortnightly expense. Put it aside and don't touch it.

**Subscription creep.** The average household is paying for three to five subscriptions they've forgotten about or barely use. Streaming services, app subscriptions, forgotten free trials that converted to paid. A five-minute audit of your bank statements once a quarter catches all of these.

**Mixing bills money with spending money.** If your mortgage, rent, and utilities payments live in the same account as your day-to-day spending, you will overdraw it at some point. Open a second account. Direct the bills there automatically. What's left in the main account is what you have to spend. Simple, and it works.

**Doing it once and stopping.** The value of the reset is not in the first session. It's in the pattern. One fortnight of clarity is better than nothing, but it won't change your position. Ten fortnights in a row will.

**Trying to fix everything at once.** People sit down, identify six financial problems, then try to solve all six simultaneously. They burn out within a week and go back to avoiding it. Fix one thing at a time. Get one win. Then move to the next. Small wins repeated often become big results.

## Where to start

Pick a night this week. It doesn't have to be a big event. Fifteen minutes at the kitchen table with a coffee. Write down what's coming in. Write down what's going out. See how they compare.

That's one fortnight. Do it again in two weeks.

Most people who try this tell us later that the first session was the hardest part, and the numbers were never as bad as they'd been imagining. Sometimes they are as bad as imagined, but even then, knowing is better. You can only fix what you can see.

The reset takes ten minutes. Start with one fortnight.
